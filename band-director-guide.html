Band Director's Practical Guide

Concrete, Repeatable Strategies for Building Your Program

Hosted by Dr. Jonathan Hinkle and Mr. Andrew Bajorek

I. Philosophy & Framing

1. Define the Core Value Proposition of Your Program

Quality = Fun: "It's fun to be good" → frame hard work as the path to the most rewarding kind of fun.

Explicitly teach students (and parents/admin) that band's purpose includes:

  • Teamwork and leadership
  • Discipline and time management
  • Long-term commitment and resilience

2. Respect First, Likeability Second

  • Establish: "I want you to respect me first; if you end up liking me, that's a bonus."
  • Make expectations and boundaries explicit from day one.
  • Follow through consistently so students know you are safe, fair, and predictable.

3. Play to the Long Game (5-Year Horizon)

  • Normalize that it takes ~5 years to truly stop being "the new director."
  • Track progress by cohorts (e.g., "Class of 2029") instead of by single-year snapshots.
  • Document what you try each year and your perceived impact (brief notes, not a novel).

II. Recruitment: Getting Them In the Door

A. Feeder Programs & Vertical Alignment

1. Regular, Low-Pressure Presence at Middle Schools

Use planning periods to:

  • Show up, hang out, and be visible—not just "come in to fix them."
  • Greet students by name; circulate, listen, and give small bits of positive feedback.
Strategy: Aim for consistent presence (e.g., weekly, biweekly) over big, infrequent events.

2. Side-by-Side Experiences

Organize:

  • Football game nights with 8th graders sitting and playing with HS band.
  • Parade/pep events where MS and HS students combine.

Provide:

  • Clear schedule
  • Simple but exciting music
  • A quick social element (pizza, snacks, games, or movie afterward).

3. Leverage Elementary/Younger Students

Bring select HS or MS players to elementary events:

  • Demonstrations of instruments
  • Simple joint pieces or call-and-response activities

Follow up with a flyer or QR code to your band's social media or website.

B. Student "Social Magnets" & Peer Influence

1. Identify Social Connectors

Look for:

  • Students everyone knows or gravitates toward.
  • Athletes, artists, "smart kids," and kids embedded in different social groups.

Invite them into visible roles even if they're not your best players:

  • Section leaders
  • Drumline/pep band anchors
  • Student hosts on recruitment nights

2. Peer-to-Peer Recruiting

Train a student ambassador team:

  • Give them 2–3 key talking points about why they stay in band.
  • Ask them to visit 7th/8th grade classes regularly (once a quarter or more).
  • Strategically match ambassadors to feeder classrooms that reflect their own background or interests (athletes to athlete-heavy schools, etc.).

3. Student-Led Q&A Panels

During recruitment nights:

  • Step back and let a small panel of HS students take questions from MS students.
  • Provide a few seed questions (e.g., "How do you balance band and sports?").

III. Retention: Keeping Them in the Program

A. Structural & Cultural Retention

1. Continuity of People

  • Advocate for consistent staffing at feeder schools when you can.
  • If there is turnover:
    • Offer to co-plan concerts, join rehearsals, and be a familiar face for students.
  • Share your calendar and goals with new feeder teachers early.

2. Strong Internal Culture

Treat your ensemble like an intentional community:

  • Teach norms: how rehearsals begin, how to respond to feedback, how to welcome new members.
  • Build rituals (e.g., pre-concert circle, weekly "shout-outs," end-of-season reflections).

Celebrate:

  • Individual and group progress, not just performances or ratings.

B. Instructional Approach & Student Experience

1. Balancing Technical Rigor and Emotional Climate

  • Be explicit: "We will work hard and also acknowledge when we improve."
  • After a successful run:
    • Stop and say, "We did that correctly—listen to the difference."
Separate: Rehearsal time = work, Non-rehearsal time (breaks, transitions, bus rides) = relationship-building.

2. Structured Rehearsals

  • Start exactly on time (e.g., 3:00 means music in hand and ready to play).
  • Have:
    • A posted plan for the day (3–4 bullets).
    • Clear warm-up sequence and expectations.
  • End with a win:
    • A good run-through
    • A moment to reflect on what improved that day.

3. Avoiding Over-Saturation

Set limits on:

  • After-school "open band room" hours.
  • Extra rehearsals and voluntary band-only hang time.
Sometimes send kids home on purpose: "We're done—go be with family, friends, sports, or just rest."

C. Key Transitions (MS → HS, 9th → 10–12th)

1. Ninth-Grade Onboarding

Provide:

  • A "Welcome to HS Band" mini-handbook.
  • A buddy/mentor system pairing each freshman with an upperclassman.

Run a specific 9th-grade orientation rehearsal before school starts if possible.

2. Sustaining 10–12th Grade Enrollment

For juniors/seniors:

  • Offer leadership roles (section leader, librarian, social media, equipment).
  • Create opportunities for them to design parts of the experience (themes, show ideas, outreach events).

Make band a value-add alongside college classes:

  • Flexible scheduling where possible.
  • Options for credit (e.g., independent study, dual-enrollment coordination if allowed).

IV. Scheduling & Competing Demands (Sports, AP, Work)

A. Collaborating with Coaches

1. Proactive Coach Relationships

At the start of the year:

  • Meet or email every head coach whose season overlaps with band.
  • Share major dates and ask for theirs.
Win–Win Compromise: "If you can release them 15 minutes early on Tuesdays, I'll allow a 10–15 minute late arrival to rehearsal those days."

2. Joint Scheduling Agreements

For overlapping practices:

  • Establish a shared schedule (e.g., sports 3:00–4:15, band 4:30–6:00).

Communicate these clearly to:

  • Students
  • Parents
  • Administration

3. Use Coaches as Allies

Whenever possible, ask supportive coaches to:

  • Publicly endorse band participation.
  • Acknowledge band kids at team meetings.

B. Working with Counselors & Admin

1. Counselor Relationship-Building

Make a habit of:

  • Dropping off coffee/donuts at key times (scheduling season, start of year).
  • Sending a one-page explainer:
    • Required sequence (Band I–IV, jazz, etc.)
    • How band fits in 4-year plans (with AP/college classes).
  • Ask to be on or at least consulted by any scheduling committee.

2. Pre-Registration Advocacy

Before registration:

  • Provide counselors with lists of current band students and recommended pathways.
  • Send families:
    • Sample 4-year schedules (band + AP + sports).

Host an info night about "How to keep band in your schedule."

3. Support Students with Work/Family Obligations

Offer:

  • Clear attendance policies that acknowledge jobs and family duties.
  • Alternate ways to earn some credit (chamber groups, community gigs, reflection projects) if your policies and district allow.

V. Programming for Small or Unbalanced Ensembles

A. Repertoire Strategy

1. Limit Instrumentation on Purpose

Instead of chasing a "full" concert band you don't have:

  • Choose literature that fits your actual roster (flex-band, chamber, jazz-style groups).
  • Consider "chamber wind ensemble" branding for small, auditioned groups.

2. Re-Scoring Intentionally

With students:

  • Look at full band charts and decide who will cover missing parts.
  • Try alternate scorings in rehearsal and pick what sounds best.

Encourage:

  • Student input on who should take key lines ("Who wants the horn melody?").

3. Alternative Formats

Pilot:

  • Jazz/rock-driven ensembles using the instruments you do have.
  • Percussion ensembles or groove-based drumline to be your public "hook."
Use these as visible flagship groups that draw interest and then funnel students into other ensembles.

B. Visibility & Performance Opportunities

1. High-Impact, Low-Pressure Performances

Aim for:

  • Community events (parades, pep gigs, local festivals).
  • Short, engaging sets instead of only formal concerts.

Showcase:

  • Pieces that sound good with your group, even if non-traditional.

2. Strategic Trips

If big trips are financially unrealistic:

  • Plan smaller, more frequent experiences (in-state festivals, day trips, joint concerts).

When you do travel:

  • Brand it as a multi-year build (e.g., "2027 Tour" with fundraising starting early).
  • Avoid a strict "trip year/non-trip year" cycle; vary timing to reduce boom–bust enrollment.

VI. Community & Relationship Building

A. Parents & Community Partners

1. Presence at Feeder and Community Events

Attend:

  • Middle school concerts
  • Local parades
  • Community performances involving your students

Be visible:

  • Greet parents
  • Thank feeder directors publicly

2. Parent Engagement

Establish:

  • A simple parent group or booster (even if very small).

Involve them in:

  • Chaperoning
  • Food for events
  • Simple fundraisers

B. Student Ownership

1. Student Committees

Create small teams for:

  • Social events
  • Recruitment (posters, videos, middle-school visits)
  • Spirit/culture (recognitions, birthday board, etc.)

Give them real responsibility and follow through on their ideas when possible.

2. Student-Led Events

Encourage:

  • Student-run sectionals or "band club" after school—with your blessing, clear rules, and minimal supervision as required by policy.

Frame these as:

  • Add-ons, not replacements, for the core class ensemble.

VII. Personal Sustainability for the Director

1. Choose a Few Focus Strategies Each Year

  • Year 1–2: Visibility in feeders + basic structure and culture.
  • Year 3–4: Refined repertoire strategy + deeper counselor/coach collaboration.
  • Year 5+: Larger initiatives (trips, festivals, expanded ensembles).

2. Reflect Without Self-Blame

Each semester, ask:

  • What seemed to work?
  • What clearly didn't?
  • What will I keep, tweak, or drop?
Use data where possible:
  • Enrollment numbers by grade
  • Retention between 8–9, 9–10, etc.
  • Simple student surveys (but interpret them through your professional lens).

Saxophone Technique Workshop

Hosted by NSSS Quartet

Dr. Stephen Page · Dr. Shawna Pennock · Dr. Nathan Mertens · Dr. Sarah Hetrick

1. Teaching Tone at Soft Dynamics

Core Concept

Softer does not mean "less support"; it means:

  • Lower air volume
  • Maintained / high air speed via tongue position and oral cavity shape

Actionable Strategies

Use language like:

  • "Less air volume, same air speed."
  • "Compress the air with your tongue, don't just back off."

With students, have them:

  1. Play mf with a full, centered sound.
  2. Gradually decrease volume while raising the tongue (EEE position) to keep the tone focused.
  3. Listen for "air in the sound" and label it: "Your air is too slow; compress more with tongue/inside of mouth."

Quick Checks

If the sound is airy at soft dynamics:

  • Ask: "Where is your tongue? Say 'eee' inside your mouth."
  • Listen for reed noise/air → remind: "Faster air, not just less air."

2. Air, "Support," and Body Engagement

Reframe "Support" → "Engagement"

  • Use words like engage, activate, or air energy instead of the vague "support."
  • Emphasize body understanding, not just verbal metaphors.

Straw Exercise (Core Strategy)

Use very thin coffee stir straws.

Drill:
  1. Students blow through the straw at a "normal" air level.
  2. Ask them to "hit you with the air" through the straw (more intensity).
  3. Stop and ask: "Where did your body engage?"
  4. Have them feel front/side/back of torso with hands.
  5. Label that feeling as "good engagement" and reference it when they play.

"Dog Pant" Exercise

Have students put a hand on the low ribs/side/back and pant like a dog:

  • Feel obliques and back muscles fire.
  • Connect this to articulation and accents: "Use the same engagement you feel when panting to power your tonguing and accents."

Breathing Cues

  • Focus on rib expansion, not "stick your stomach out": "Breathe so your ribs expand in 360°."
  • Avoid shaming language around the belly; talk about rib cage motion and tall breathing.

3. Reed Selection, Strength, and Matching to Mouthpiece

Matching, Not "Stronger Is Better"

  • Teach: reed choice = matching mouthpiece + environment + player, not ego.
  • General rule of thumb:
    • More open mouthpiece → softer reed
    • More closed mouthpiece → can handle slightly harder reed

Altitude & Climate

  • At higher altitudes/dry climates: Expect to use slightly softer reeds than at sea level.
  • Normalize this so students don't see softer reeds as a "downgrade."

Talking to Students

Explicitly counter the myth:
  • "Stronger reed ≠ better player."
  • "The best reed is the one that lets you play in tune, with control, across dynamics."

4. Reed Rotation, Storage, and Wetting

Rotation System

For high schoolers, recommend:

  • 5–8 reeds in active rotation.
  • Never more than ~20 minutes on a single reed when practicing intensely.
  • For concerts: Have a "top 2–3 reeds" ready, but still rotated in daily use.

Storage & Humidity

  • Advocate flat, ventilated or humidity-controlled cases.
  • Airtight reed cases with humidity packs around 58–72% RH help massively in dry climates.
  • Practical classroom note: Have at least one good reed case example to show.
  • Encourage families to invest in one quality case rather than a constant stream of emergency reeds.

Wetting Reeds: Water, Not Spit

Teach students to use water cups or tiny bottles rather than saliva.
  • Saliva enzymes break down the cane faster.
  • Simple policy: "Reeds get water, not spit."

Diagnosing & Fixing Warp

Show students:

  • Flip reed over → if it "rocking chairs" or has a horseshoe bump, the back is warped.
  • Easy band-room fix: Lay the reed flat on clean white paper and make a few light, flat strokes to help flatten fibers after playing.
  • Then store in a flat case so it doesn't become a "potato chip."

5. Practical Gear Recommendations

(Emphasize: consistency and match > brand "status.")

Reeds

  • D'Addario (Rico, Reserve, etc.)
    • Known for consistent manufacturing and good customer support.
    • Official: https://www.daddario.com/products/woodwinds/
  • Vandoren "blue box" and similar are still common and fine; just stress quality control and rotation.

Mouthpieces

For step-up/classical-focused students:

  • Stable choice: Selmer C*-type and analogous designs (if your store has reliable stock).
  • Highlight newer CNC-machined student mouthpieces (like the CSX-style models mentioned in the session) for consistency and reasonable price.
  • Key message: upgrade to one good, consistent mouthpiece before obsessing over exotic reeds.

Ligatures

  • Recommend simple, durable metal ligatures with reliable screws (e.g., Rovner-type or standard metal; avoid very cheap, flimsy stamped ligatures).
  • Emphasize:
    • Ligature should hold the reed evenly, not crush it.
    • A solid $30–$50 ligature is a small investment with an outsized effect on ease of playing and lasts for years.

6. Day-to-Day Reed & Setup Habits

Make these into posted classroom rules:

Assembly Order

Mount reed on mouthpiece first, then on neck/body:
  1. Mouthpiece + ligature → reed placement → then attach to neck/sax.

Line-up Check

  • "A hairline of black": enough tip opening that a single hair laid across would just be covered by the tip of the reed.
  • Too much mouthpiece showing = muffled, unfocused sound.

Tenor & Bari Rule

If they say it feels like a "2x4," check:

  • Reed warp
  • Reed strength vs. mouthpiece opening
  • Moisture and storage

Retire Reeds When

  • Dynamic range has shrunk.
  • Tip chirps/squeaks from being too soft at the tip.
  • Reed has gone fully "potato chip" despite flattening attempts.

7. Embouchure and Inside-the-Mouth Work

Inside the Mouth: Tongue Height & Frontness

Use syllables:

  • For warmer, controlled sound: think "eee" (high, front tongue).
  • For students with bright/harsh/edgy tone, they may be:
    • Low tongue, far back ("aw" inside the mouth)
    • Overcompensating with jaw pressure instead of tongue/air shaping.
Simple prompt: "Say 'eee' inside your mouth as you play that note again."

Embouchure Language

  • Avoid "pressure"; use: "Necessary engagement of the corners and top lip."
  • "Corners in and forward, not down/biting."

Quick Tests

  • Have them form embouchure without instrument, then add mouthpiece.
  • Use analogies like the "French cow" (moo → "meu") to get the upper lip engaged and lifted.

8. Posture and Instrument Positioning

Simple, Repeatable Cues

Use a chant (especially with younger players):

"Feet flat, back tall, knees on the hump."

  • Replace "sit up straight" with: "Sit tall," "Find your sit bones," "String lifting the crown of your head."

Saxophone Position

Core rule: Instrument goes to the body, not body to instrument.

For Alto

  • Move to center position as soon as they're tall enough that: Right hand clears thighs without the wrist bending downwards.

For Neck Straps

  • Adjust strap while student is already sitting tall.
  • Mouthpiece should meet the mouth without the student dropping the head or jutting the chin.

9. Section Management: Tenor & Bari

Pairing & Seating

Whenever possible:

  • Put two students on tenor and bari parts.
  • Or at least seat them near each other so they can hear a "sectional buddy."

Rationale

  • These parts are often under-supported and harmonically different; players tend to underplay.
  • A buddy setup = more confidence, better pitch and style consistency.

Sound Concept

Encourage them to listen to professional tenor and bari recordings and copy:

  • Warmth
  • Core to the sound
  • Connection to the rest of the sax section.

10. Using Recordings and Video to Shape Sound Concepts

Showing the "Right Sound" in Class

Build a short playlist of high-level classical and contemporary saxophonists:

Examples mentioned:
  • Nobuya Sugawa
  • Steven Page
  • Steven Banks
  • Your session clinicians' own quartet and solo videos (highly produced YouTube content)

Strategy

  • Use short clips (30–60 seconds) at the start of class or sectionals.
  • Ask:
    • "Describe this tone in 3 words."
    • "How focused is the sound? How warm? How clear is the articulation?"

Homework & Listening

  • Give students specific search phrases, e.g.: "Search YouTube for 'Steven Banks saxophone' and pick one piece to describe."
Emphasize: "You can't sound like what you've never heard."

11. Session-Based "Next Week" Action Plan for Directors

This Week

  • Introduce straw exercise to full band.
  • Post a "Reed Care Checklist" in the band room: water, rotation, case, warp check.

Within 2 Weeks

  • Audit sax section setups:
    • Neck strap height
    • Mouthpiece placement
    • Tenor/bari seating & pairing
  • Have saxes bring all active reeds → help them build a 5–8 reed rotation.

Within 1 Month

  • Recommend or bulk-order:
    • 1–2 consistent student mouthpiece models for step-up players.
    • At least one good ligature model to suggest to parents.
  • Start a listening log: 1 short professional sax clip per week.
